# Where to go next

This module is an introduction. It is deliberately wide and deliberately shallow, and by the end of
it you should be able to tell which direction you want to go deeper in.

Everything below is real, current and reachable. Where something is free, it says so. Where it
costs money, it says roughly what for, because a certificate you cannot afford this year is still
worth knowing exists.

**One honest warning about certificates.** In this industry, almost nobody is hired for a
certificate. They are hired for what they can do, and a certificate is a way of proving you have
done the reading when nobody has seen you work yet. That makes them most useful early, and the free
ones are worth doing on that basis alone.

## Block 1: Free, start this month

These cost nothing and they map directly onto what this module covered.

| What | Who runs it | Follows from | Why it is worth it |
|------|-------------|--------------|--------------------|
| **Dante Certification, Levels 1 to 3** | Audinate, `audinate.com` | Class 5 | The industry standard for networked audio, free, online, self paced. Level 1 and 2 are genuinely introductory. Turns up in job adverts. |
| **Networking Basics / Networking Essentials** | Cisco Networking Academy, `netacad.com` | Class 3 | Free, and far more thorough than one class can be. Do it if the subnetting was the part you enjoyed. |
| **Q-SYS Level 1 training** | QSC, `qsc.com` | Classes 4 and 5 | Free online, and one of the clearest introductions to networked control and audio over IP as a system. |
| **ETC training resources** | ETC, `etcconnect.com` | Class 4 | Free tutorials on Eos family consoles, and their networking documentation is unusually honest about failure modes. |
| **Ben Eater, building an 8 bit computer** | `eater.net` | Class 2 | If "what a computer actually is" was the part that landed, this is that question answered at the level of individual wires. Free, and outstanding. |
| **The IETF RFCs** | `rfc-editor.org` | Class 3 | Every internet protocol, free, forever. RFC 791 is IP and RFC 768 is UDP. Dry, short, and the actual source. |

## Block 3: The standards themselves

Reading a standard is a skill, and it is worth practising once on something short before you need
it under pressure. **Start with an ESTA document**, because they are free and they are written for
our industry.

| Body | Where | What is there | Free? |
|------|-------|---------------|-------|
| **ESTA Technical Standards Program** | `tsp.esta.org` | ANSI E1.11 (DMX512-A), E1.20 (RDM), E1.31 (sACN), and the rigging and electrical standards | **Yes, free downloads** |
| **AES** | `aes.org` | AES3, AES67, AES70. Also a large free library of conference papers. | Standards paid, many papers free |
| **SMPTE** | `smpte.org` | ST 2110, ST 12 (timecode), ST 2059 (PTP profile) | Paid, and some free overviews |
| **IEEE** | `standards.ieee.org` | 802.1 (VLANs, TSN, AVB), 802.3 (Ethernet), 1588 (PTP) | Paid, with some free access programmes |
| **showstack** | `showstack-inky.vercel.app` | Which standard covers what, whether it is free to read, and a citation on every entry | **Yes, free, open data** |

## Block 4: Books, which age better than links

A short list, all of them standard references rather than fashionable ones.

- **Charles Poynton, *Digital Video and HD: Algorithms and Interfaces*.** The definitive treatment
  of gamma, luma, chroma and colour for video. If the colour material interested you, this is where
  it goes next, and it is rigorous without being unreadable.
- **Bob McCarthy, *Sound Systems: Design and Optimization*.** How sound behaves in rooms, and how
  systems are aligned. The reference for anyone going towards system engineering.
- **Andrew Tanenbaum, *Computer Networks*.** The textbook. Heavier than you need for a show
  network, and it will make Class 3 feel like the introduction it was.
- **Brad Schiller, *The Automated Lighting Programmer's Handbook*.** Practical, specific, and it
  assumes you are going to be in a production office at midnight.
- **Al Kovalick, *Video Systems in an IT Environment*.** Video as data rather than as pictures,
  which is exactly the transition Class 5 is about.
- **Nisan and Schocken, *The Elements of Computing Systems*** (the Nand2Tetris book). Build a
  working computer from logic gates upward. The most satisfying way to make Class 2 permanent.

## Block 5: Where the working conversations happen

Reading is half of it. The other half is watching people argue about real problems.

- **The Lighting Network** and **Blue Room** forums, for lighting and production electrics.
- **Sound on Sound** forums for audio, and the **AES** conference papers for the serious end.
- **Manufacturer forums and Discord servers** for the products you actually use. disguise, ETC,
  MA Lighting, Resolume and QLab all have active communities where the answers to genuinely odd
  questions live.
- **Your venue's technical staff.** The single most underused resource in this list, and the only
  one that knows what is actually installed where you are.

## Block 6: How to choose a direction

Three honest questions, and the answers are not equal.

**Did you like finding the fault, or building the thing?** Fault finding leads towards systems
engineering, network design and technical direction. Building leads towards design, programming and
content. Both are careers, and people are usually much better at one.

**Did the arithmetic bother you or please you?** If subnetting and data rates were satisfying, the
networking road is open and it is well paid. If they were a chore you tolerated to get to the
creative work, that is useful information and not a failing.

**Which department's problem did you want to solve?** Notice which of the five classes you wanted
to keep reading. That is a better guide than any careers advice, and it is available to you right
now for free.

> Nobody in this industry learned it in the order it is taught. Pick the thread you actually want
> to pull, and the rest fills in around it faster than you would expect.
